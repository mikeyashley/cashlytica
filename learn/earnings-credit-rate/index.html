Earnings Credit Rate (ECR): Formula and Example

An earnings credit rate (ECR) is an imputed rate a bank applies to eligible business deposit balances to calculate a credit against certain account-analysis charges. It is a fee allowance, not cash interest paid to the company.

The credit depends on more than the quoted rate. The bank’s eligible balance definition, adjustments, day-count convention, covered fees, and treatment of unused credits all affect the amount actually applied.

How to calculate the monthly ECR credit

A common account-analysis structure is:

Estimated credit = average eligible collected balance × (1 − balance adjustment) × ECR × days in period ÷ day-count basis

Banks use different contract terms and statement conventions. Use the exact balance base, adjustments, rate, and day-count rule shown in your bank’s analysis statement.

Illustrative example only: assume $1,000,000 of average eligible collected balance, a 10% balance adjustment, a 1.50% annual ECR, and a 30-day month using a 365-day basis.

Eligible balance after 10% adjustment$900,000
Calculated ECR credit$900,000 × 1.50% × 30 ÷ 365 = $1,109.59
Analyzed service charges$1,500.00
Estimated amount remaining after credit$390.41

This example does not describe any bank’s current rate or contract. If eligible credits exceed charges, ask whether the excess expires, carries forward, or is limited to particular services.

ECR is not the same as deposit interest

The credit generally offsets eligible service fees on the analysis statement; it is not necessarily paid to the business as withdrawable interest. Compare the full economics: credit earned, service fees, minimum or compensating balance, liquidity constraints, and what the bank pays on any interest-bearing alternative. A higher ECR does not automatically mean a lower total banking cost.

What to check on an account-analysis statement

  1. Average ledger balance, collected balance, and any float or balance adjustment.
  2. The eligible balance used to calculate the credit and the ECR applied for that period.
  3. The day-count basis and the date or event that changes the rate.
  4. Gross analyzed charges before the credit, including service volumes and unit prices.
  5. Credit applied, unused credit, carry-forward or expiry rules, and residual amount billed.
  6. Any minimum-balance, tier, account, or relationship requirement in the treasury agreement.

The Association for Financial Professionals describes ECR as imputed interest and notes that account-analysis methods and tiers vary. Your agreement and statement control the actual calculation.
