How to Audit Bank Fees: A Guide for Finance Teams

Most mid-market companies overpay their banks by thousands of dollars annually. A bank fee audit identifies exactly where — typically in wire transfer fees, account service charges, and analysis fees.

What is a bank fee audit?

A bank fee audit is a systematic review of your bank statements to identify fees that are above market rate, incorrectly applied, or duplicated. Banks charge dozens of individual fee lines — wire fees, ACH fees, account maintenance fees, lockbox fees, positive pay fees, and more — and most finance teams never review them line by line.

A proper audit compares your actual fees against published bank tariffs and industry benchmarks, then flags the lines where you're paying more than you should.

The most common sources of bank fee leakage

  • Wire transfer fees above benchmark — domestic wires typically run $15–$25. Anything above $35 is worth questioning.
  • Account analysis fee miscalculations — banks compute these monthly using an earnings credit rate (ECR). If your ECR is below the Fed funds rate, you're subsidizing the bank.
  • Duplicate service charges — common after bank mergers or account restructurings where legacy fee codes persist.
  • Dormant service fees — fees for services (positive pay, remote deposit capture) that the company stopped using but never deactivated.
  • Negotiated rate drift — your contract locked in rates two years ago; the bank quietly increased them on non-contract lines.

How to run a bank fee audit manually

To audit bank fees without a tool:

  1. Download 3–6 months of bank analysis statements (these are separate from your account statements).
  2. Export each fee line into a spreadsheet: fee code, description, volume, unit rate, total.
  3. Request your bank's current fee schedule and compare against your actual charges.
  4. Flag any line where your rate exceeds the published rate or where volume appears inconsistent with your activity.
  5. Request a credit or renegotiate the lines with the largest dollar variance.

This process typically takes 4–8 hours for a company with 3–5 bank accounts.
